# Objection playbook

Each category: what it usually means underneath, what to check before drafting,
the shape of a good response, and the failure mode to avoid.

---

## "Too expensive"

**Usually means** the value is not yet worth the number, or they are comparing
against a cheaper alternative you have not surfaced. Rarely means the number
itself is impossible.

**Check first** whether they have seen a number at all, and what they are
comparing against. "Expensive" before a proposal is a different objection.

**Shape**
- Acknowledge without discounting: price is a fair thing to push on.
- Reframe to the cost of the current state, using their own numbers if research found any.
- Ask what they are comparing it against.

**Do not** offer a discount, hint at flexibility, or defend the price with a
feature list. Discounting on the first push teaches the buyer to push again.

---

## "Not the right time" / "revisit next quarter"

**Usually means** no urgency, no internal champion, or a real cycle you cannot
move. The first two are workable, the third is not.

**Check first** for a trigger in the brief that contradicts the timing — a
funding round, a hiring wave, a compliance deadline.

**Shape**
- Accept the timing at face value; do not argue it.
- Name the trigger that made you reach out now, briefly.
- Ask what would need to be true for it to be worth looking at sooner.

**Do not** fake urgency or invent a deadline. Ask for the specific date to
re-engage and honour it.

---

## "We're already using {competitor}"

**Usually means** switching costs feel higher than the upside. Sometimes means
they are unhappy and testing whether you know the difference.

**Check first** `references/proof-points.md` for documented positioning against
that competitor. If it is a placeholder, you have nothing to say here yet.

**Shape**
- Assume the incumbent is a reasonable choice, and say so.
- One documented, specific difference. Not a category, a behaviour.
- Ask what they would change about the current setup if it were free to do so.

**Do not** disparage, and do not claim parity on things you cannot show. The
prospect knows the incumbent better than you do.

---

## "No budget"

**Usually means** no budget *line*, which is a sponsorship problem, not a money
problem. Distinguish it from "too expensive" — this one is about access.

**Check first** whether the contact has ever owned a purchase, and who does.

**Shape**
- Acknowledge that unbudgeted things need a sponsor.
- Ask how something like this normally gets funded there — a cycle, a discretionary
  pool, an existing line it could displace.
- Offer to give them what they would need to make the case internally.

**Do not** ask to be introduced to their boss in the first reply. Earn the path.

---

## "Send me some information"

**Usually means** a polite exit, occasionally a real request from someone who
has to brief others.

**Check first** whether anything in the thread suggests a real internal audience.

**Shape**
- Send something specific and short, not a deck.
- Attach one question that only matters if they are actually interested.
- Make it easy to say no, which makes a yes worth something.

**Do not** send a generic overview and mark it as progress.

---

## "I'm not the right person"

**Usually means** exactly that, and it is the cheapest objection to resolve.

**Shape**
- Thank them, ask who owns it, ask permission to mention their name.
- Keep it to two sentences.

**Do not** pitch the wrong person harder. Do not add the referred name to the
CRM without the rep deciding to.

---

## Status quo ("we handle this manually and it's fine")

**Usually means** the pain is real but distributed, so no one owns it.

**Shape**
- Take the current process seriously; it works, or they would have replaced it.
- Ask what it costs when volume doubles, or when the person who owns it is away.
- Let them do the arithmetic. Do not do it for them out loud.

**Do not** tell them their process is broken. They built it.
